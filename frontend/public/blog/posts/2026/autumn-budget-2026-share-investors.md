The Autumn Budget is on **28 October 2026**, and Capital Gains Tax (CGT) is one of the most discussed areas of possible reform. If you hold shares or ETFs outside an ISA or pension, this is the week to understand where you stand, before the headlines start telling you what to do.

This article separates three things: what is **already in force**, what is **only being reported or speculated**, and what you can **sensibly do now**. We will update it once the Chancellor has spoken.

*This is general information, not tax or investment advice. Check figures against GOV.UK or a qualified adviser before acting.*

## What is already in force for 2026/27

None of these depend on the Budget:

- **CGT rates on shares: 18% and 24%.** Gains that fall within your basic rate band are taxed at 18%, and anything above it at 24%. These rates have applied to shares since the October 2024 Budget.
- **Annual exempt amount: £3,000.** It was £12,300 only three tax years ago, so far more people now have a reportable or taxable gain than before.
- **Dividend tax rose in April 2026.** The basic rate is 10.75% and the higher rate is 35.75%. The additional rate stays at 39.35%, and the dividend allowance remains £500.
- **Matching rules still apply.** Disposals are matched against same-day purchases first, then purchases in the following 30 days, then your Section 104 pool.

## What is being reported, not confirmed

Treat all of the following as speculation until the Budget statement and the published documents confirm it:

- **Higher CGT rates.** Early pre-Budget reports suggested CGT could rise. Other reports say the Chancellor has ruled out raising rates this time. The two cannot both be right, which is exactly why guessing is risky.
- **Aligning CGT with income tax rates.** Some commentators claimed this would raise around £14 billion. The director of the Institute for Fiscal Studies has challenged that figure, citing HMRC estimates that even a 10% CGT increase could *cost* the Exchequer about £3.5 billion because people sell less.
- **Reform without a rate rise.** The Chartered Institute of Taxation has flagged CGT as heavily discussed. Reported ideas include removing the CGT uplift on death, which would be a change in rules rather than rates.

## Why HMRC attention on CGT matters now

HMRC closed about **9,800 investigations into underpaid CGT in 2024-25**, up 26% on the previous year, recovering roughly **£266 million**. The average underpayment was around **£27,000** per case. With the allowance at £3,000, more disposals are now above the line where they must be reported, so accurate records are no longer optional.

## A simple example of what a rate change would do

Say you are a higher-rate taxpayer and realise a £20,000 gain in 2026/27:

| | Today (24%) | Illustrative 28% |
|---|---|---|
| Gain | £20,000 | £20,000 |
| Less annual exempt amount | £3,000 | £3,000 |
| Taxable gain | £17,000 | £17,000 |
| Tax due | **£4,080** | £4,760 |

The 28% column is purely an illustration of sensitivity, not a prediction. The point is that a four-point change on a £17,000 taxable gain is worth £680, which matters, but not enough to justify rushed decisions that carry their own costs.

## Be careful with "sell before the Budget"

Selling before 28 October only helps if you genuinely want to be out of the position. Watch out for:

- **The 30-day rule.** If you sell and buy the same shares back within 30 days, the disposal is matched with the new purchase, not your old cost. You get no reset, and your gain can come out very different from what you expected.
- **Any change would normally apply from the Budget date or a later date.** The October 2024 Budget changed CGT rates with immediate effect on the day, so waiting until after the announcement may be too late to act, and acting before it may be unnecessary. You cannot know in advance.
- **Dealing costs and market risk.** Selling a good holding to save a few hundred pounds of possible tax can easily cost more than it saves.

## What you can sensibly do this week

1. **Know your position.** Work out your realised gains and losses so far this tax year, and your unrealised gains per holding. Our [free calculator](/calculator) handles same-day, 30-day and Section 104 matching from your broker export.
2. **Use allowances that do not depend on the Budget.** Your ISA allowance, pension contributions, and your spouse's or civil partner's allowance and basic-rate band can all reduce tax whatever happens.
3. **Harvest losses deliberately.** Realised losses can be set against gains in the same year, and you must report them to carry them forward. See our guide to [tax-loss harvesting](/blog/post/tax-loss-harvesting-strategy).
4. **Check your records.** Keep trade confirmations, FX rates and fees. HMRC's scrutiny of CGT is rising, and a printable report of your calculation makes your Self Assessment return much easier to defend.
5. **Wait for the detail.** Budget speeches summarise. The real rules, start dates and any transitional provisions appear in the published documents afterwards.
