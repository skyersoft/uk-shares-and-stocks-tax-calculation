Most people give to charity because they care, not because of the tax. But the tax system quietly pays part of your gift for you, and the more you earn, the bigger that share is. Many higher earners never claim what they are owed.

This guide covers the two main ways to give from your pay, what each does to your take-home money, and what you need to calculate for your Self Assessment return. The figures are for **2026/27 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland**. Scotland has different income tax rates, so your relief differs slightly.

### The numbers that matter

| Band | Rate | Income (2026/27) |
|------|------|------------------|
| Basic | 20% | up to £50,270 |
| Higher | 40% | £50,271 - £125,140 |
| Additional | 45% | over £125,140 |

The thresholds are frozen, so pay rises keep pulling more people into the 40% band. That makes charitable giving worth more to more people each year.

### Route 1: Payroll Giving (the automatic one)

If your employer runs a Payroll Giving scheme, your donation comes out of your pay **before income tax is worked out**. You get the relief at your own top rate straight away, with nothing to claim.

What a **£100 donation** costs you out of take-home pay:

| You pay tax at | Cost to you | Charity receives |
|----------------|-------------|------------------|
| 20% | £80 | £100 |
| 40% | £60 | £100 |
| 45% | £55 | £100 |

**A worked example.** You earn £60,000 and give £200 a month (£2,400 a year) through payroll:

- Your taxable pay falls to £57,600, and all of the £2,400 would have been taxed at 40%.
- You save £960 in income tax.
- Your take-home pay drops by only **£1,440 a year (£120 a month)**.
- The charity receives the full **£2,400**.

Payroll Giving reduces your income tax only. National Insurance is still charged on your full pay.

### Route 2: Gift Aid (the one you have to claim)

When you give from money you have already paid tax on, the charity adds 25p for every £1 you give by reclaiming the basic-rate tax. You give £80, the charity gets £100.

If you pay 40% or 45%, **that is only half the story**. The extra relief, the difference between the basic rate and your rate, belongs to you, but HMRC will not give it to you automatically. You claim it through Self Assessment or by asking HMRC to change your tax code.

**The same £60,000 earner** gives £2,400 by Gift Aid:

- The charity receives £3,000 (your £2,400 plus £600 from HMRC).
- You reclaim another 20% of £3,000, which is **£600**.
- Your own cost is £1,800 for a £3,000 gift. That is still 60p per £1 the charity receives, the same as payroll.

Skip the claim and you pay 80p for each £1 the charity receives instead of 60p, and the £600 stays with HMRC.

### Why you should calculate your donations for Self Assessment

1. **The higher-rate relief is only paid if you claim it.** Put your Gift Aid donations on your return, as the *gross* amount (what you gave divided by 0.8).
2. **You can move a donation back a year.** If you donate after 5 April but before you file, you can ask for it to count in the previous tax year. That is useful if last year's income was higher and so was your rate.
3. **Your total income is lowered where it matters most.** Gift Aid donations reduce your *adjusted net income*, which decides three things:
   - the **High Income Child Benefit Charge**, which runs from £60,000 to £80,000;
   - the **tapering of your personal allowance** from £100,000;
   - the point at which you hit the 45% band.
4. **Keep the records.** Note the date, charity and amount of every donation, and keep the Gift Aid confirmations. If you are using payroll giving, do not claim it again on your return. Your employer has already taken it off your taxable pay.

One condition: Gift Aid only works if, in the tax year, you have paid at least as much income tax and/or Capital Gains Tax as the charities will reclaim (25p per £1 you gave).

### The sweet spot: £100,000 to £125,140

Between £100,000 and £125,140 you lose £1 of personal allowance for every £2 of income, which creates an effective **60% tax rate**. A donation that brings your adjusted net income back down gets that relief too.

Example: you earn £110,000 and give £8,000 by Gift Aid, which is £10,000 gross.

- Your adjusted net income falls to £100,000 and your full £12,570 personal allowance returns.
- Your tax bill drops by about **£4,000** on top of the £2,000 the charity reclaims.
- You pay £8,000 and get £4,000 back, so the gift costs you **£4,000**, and the charity receives **£10,000**.

That is 40p for each £1 donated, the cheapest way to give in the whole tax system.

### Giving shares instead of cash

If you hold shares or funds that have grown in value, you can give them to a charity directly. There is **no Capital Gains Tax** on the gain, and you also get income tax relief on the full value of the gift. You claim that relief through Self Assessment. This is often the most efficient gift for investors. [Run your gains through our calculator](/calculator) first, so you know what you would have owed had you sold.

### Why give more

Tax relief does not make giving profitable: you always end up with less money than before. What it does is lower the price. If you are a 40% or 45% taxpayer, each £1 you choose to give costs you 60p or 55p, and the charity gets the full £1. For a £100,000 to £125,140 earner it costs 40p.

If you were already planning to give, check that you are claiming everything. If you have been meaning to give more, the relief means the government is covering a large share of your gift.

### Quick checklist

- [ ] Can your employer set up Payroll Giving? It is the easiest route for salaried staff.
- [ ] For other donations, ask charities for a Gift Aid declaration and keep a record of every gift.
- [ ] Add your gross Gift Aid donations to your Self Assessment return, or ask HMRC for a tax code change.
- [ ] Look at your adjusted net income. Are you near £60,000, £100,000 or £125,140?
- [ ] Consider giving shares rather than cash if you have gains.
